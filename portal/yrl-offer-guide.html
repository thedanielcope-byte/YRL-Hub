Ground rules

  • Use the current IAR/MIBOR purchase agreement. This page is strategy, not a substitute for the form. Every deadline, response period and contingency runs off the form you're actually using — read it, don't remember it.
  • A written buyer representation agreement must be in place before you tour, and certainly before you write. Your compensation is what that agreement says it is.
  • You advise; the client decides. Never tell a buyer to waive an inspection or an appraisal contingency. Lay out the risk in writing and let them choose.
  • No buyer "love letters." See the section below — this is a fair housing problem, not a charming tradition.
  • You are not a lawyer or a lender. Anything that turns into a legal or tax question goes to the appropriate professional, and you say so out loud.
  • Run anything unusual past Janet Giles-Schultz (317-997-7404) before it goes to the listing agent.

Before You Write a Word

Most weak offers are weak before anyone types a number. Five minutes on the phone with the listing agent is worth more than anything you do afterwards — and most agents never make the call.

1
Call the listing agent. Not text. Ask: how many offers do you have, when is the seller reviewing, what matters to them besides price, and is there a date they need? Half the time they'll simply tell you.
2
Confirm your buyer's financing is real. A full pre-approval with documents reviewed, not a pre-qualification off a website. Ask the lender directly if there's anything that could surprise you.
3
Check the days on market. Two days and three showings is a different offer from ninety days and one price cut. Anything sitting is a negotiation; anything fresh is a competition.
4
Pull the sold comparables yourself. If the home is overpriced you need to know before your buyer falls in love, not after the appraisal.
5
Ask your buyer the ceiling question. "If we lose this at X, will you be relieved or gutted?" The answer tells you more than a budget spreadsheet does.
Get the lender to call the listing agent. A thirty-second call from a local lender vouching for your buyer moves more listing agents than any cover letter ever written. It costs nothing and almost nobody does it.

The Seven Levers

Price gets all the attention and it is only one of seven things you control. In a multiple-offer situation, the other six are usually where the deal is actually won — and several cost your buyer nothing.

1 · Price

The obvious one. Worth remembering that on a financed purchase, the appraisal has a vote — going far above the comparables means someone has to cover the gap in cash.

2 · Earnest money

A larger deposit signals seriousness at zero real cost if the deal closes, because it credits toward the purchase. It is the cheapest way to look strong. Make sure your buyer understands the conditions under which it is and isn't refundable — that's the part that bites.

3 · Closing date

Free, and frequently decisive. A seller buying their next home may need a longer runway; an estate sale or a relocation may want speed. Ask what date they want and give it to them. This wins more deals than an extra few thousand dollars.

4 · Possession

Offering the seller a few days of post-closing possession can be worth more to them than money — it means not moving twice. Get the terms in writing, including who insures what and what happens if they overstay.

5 · Inspection approach

You have room between "full contingency" and the reckless end. Consider a shorter inspection period, or agreeing in advance to only raise items above a certain dollar threshold — so the seller knows they won't be nickel-and-dimed over a loose handrail.

Never advise waiving an inspection. You can note that a buyer may choose an inspection for information only, but the decision and the risk are theirs, and you put that in writing.

6 · Financing type and appraisal

Conventional generally reads as less friction to a seller than FHA or VA — which is not a reason to steer a buyer, just something to be honest about when a seller is comparing. If your buyer has cash reserves, a stated willingness to cover a limited appraisal gap is a powerful, specific commitment. Only offer what they can genuinely produce.

7 · Concessions and compensation

Seller-paid closing costs help a buyer who is cash-tight but rate-comfortable. Buyer-side compensation may also be requested from the seller as a term of the offer — what you may ask for is governed by your buyer representation agreement, and it is negotiated per transaction. Never present any figure as standard or customary.

Ask the listing agent which levers matter, then pull those. An offer built around what the seller actually wants beats a higher offer built around guesses, and it usually costs your buyer less.

Multiple Offers

Your job is to make sure your buyer loses on purpose or wins on purpose — never loses because you didn't ask a question you could have asked.

1
Ask how the seller is reviewing. Best-and-final? First acceptable offer? A set deadline? The process changes the strategy completely.
2
Set the ceiling before you write. Agree the walk-away number when your buyer is calm, not at 9pm when a counter arrives.
3
Make the terms clean. Fewer contingencies, realistic dates, no unnecessary requests. Every extra ask is a reason for the seller to pick someone else.
4
Give a short response deadline. Long enough to be reasonable, short enough that your offer isn't shopped around all weekend.
5
Follow up with a phone call. Confirm receipt, confirm the buyer is strong, and ask directly what it would take. Then stop talking.

Escalation clauses — careful

They can work, and they also reveal your buyer's true ceiling to the other side. Some listing agents refuse them outright and some sellers find them irritating.

If you use one, be precise about what evidence of a competing offer is required and what the cap is — and check with Janet first. A sloppy escalation clause creates a contract dispute, not a win.

Losing well matters. Thank the listing agent, and ask them to keep you in mind if it falls through — a meaningful share of accepted offers do. The agent who was gracious gets that call.

What to Avoid

Four things that feel helpful and aren't. The first one is the one most likely to cost you your license.

Buyer "love letters"

Do not write them, do not pass them along, and discourage your buyer from writing one. A letter describing the buyer's family, faith, children, or why they love the neighborhood hands the seller information about protected characteristics — and hands them a fair housing complaint if they then choose a different buyer.

They also don't work as well as agents believe. If your buyer insists, tell them plainly in writing why you won't deliver it, and check YRL's policy with Janet. Photos attached to an offer are the same problem.

Advising a buyer to waive an inspection

You can explain the options. You cannot recommend they buy a house blind, and if it goes badly, "the market was competitive" is not a defense. Put the risk in writing, let them decide, and keep the record.

Promising what you can't control

Never guarantee an appraisal will come in, that a lender will close on time, or that an inspection will be clean. Say "here's what usually happens and here's what we'll do if it doesn't."

Guessing at the form

Deadlines, response periods and remedies come from the current IAR/MIBOR agreement, and they change. Read the form for the transaction in front of you. Nobody has ever been sued for double-checking.

Presenting the Offer

A complete, well-packaged offer that arrives with a phone call is treated differently from a PDF that lands in an inbox at midnight. This part is entirely within your control.

1
Send it complete. Signed agreement, pre-approval letter, proof of funds where relevant, and any addenda. Anything missing invites delay, and delay loses deals.
2
Call to confirm it arrived. Every time. Email fails silently and listing agents are busy.
3
Summarize it in three sentences when you call — price, closing date, and the one term that makes it strong. Make it easy for them to present.
4
Ask when to expect a response, then tell your buyer that timeframe so they aren't refreshing their phone all evening.
5
Update your buyer either way. No news is still a call. It is the whole difference between a client who refers you and one who doesn't.

What to Say

Three conversations that come up on every offer.

Calling the listing agent before you write
Hi [Name], it's [Your Name] with Your Realty Link — I've got a buyer for [address] and I'd rather write something that actually works for your seller than guess. Can I ask you three quick things? Where are you on offers so far? Is there a timeline your seller needs — a date they have to be out, or want to stay to? And besides price, what would make an offer easy for them to say yes to? (Write down exactly what they say. Then build the offer around it.) That's really helpful. You'll have it from me by [time], complete, with the pre-approval attached.
Setting the ceiling with your buyer
Before we write anything, I want to ask you something and I'd like an honest answer. If we offer [X] and someone else gets it — are you relieved, or are you gutted? (Their answer is the real number. Listen to it.) Here's why I ask. In the moment, when a counter comes back at nine o'clock at night, everyone wants to go higher. I want us to decide the ceiling now, while we're both calm, so that later we're just following the plan. So what's the number where you'd rather lose it than pay it?
When they ask about waiving the inspection
I understand why you're asking — it does make an offer look stronger. Here's my honest position. I won't recommend it, and I want to be clear about why rather than just saying no. An inspection is the only point where you find out what you're actually buying. Waiving it means if there's a foundation problem, or the sewer line has failed, or the roof is at the end of its life, that becomes yours on the day you close. Those are five-figure numbers, not five-hundred-dollar ones. There are middle options. We can shorten the inspection window. We can agree to only raise items over a certain amount. Both make you look serious without you buying blind. If you decide you want to go ahead anyway, that's genuinely your call and I'll write it — I'll just put my advice in writing first so we both know where we stood.
